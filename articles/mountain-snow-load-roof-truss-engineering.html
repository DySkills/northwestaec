Mountain Snow Load Roof Truss Engineering: 60 to 120 PSF Glulam & Cold-Roof Sizing

Structural roof design for extreme ground snow loads in the Cascades, Idaho panhandle, and Montana Rockies: sizing heavy timber glulam beams, unvented SIPs, and cold-roof ventilated nail bases.

By Northwest AEC Building Science • Published October 2026 • ASCE 7-22 Chapter 7 / IRC Table R802.4.1 / AITC Timber Construction Manual
Quick Summary

In the rugged high-elevation corridors of the Cascade Range, the Idaho Panhandle, and the Montana Rockies, residential roof structures must withstand extraordinary winter environmental loads. Ground snow loads routinely range from 60 to over 120 pounds per square foot (PSF), representing tens of thousands of pounds of static and dynamic deadweight. Standard light-frame dimensional 2x4 trusses engineered for lowland coastal rain buckle under these intense loads, leading to rafter deflection, interior drywall cracking, and catastrophic structural roof collapse. Engineering safe mountain roof systems requires sizing heavy structural glued-laminated (glulam) timber beams, accommodating unbalanced drifting snow, and constructing ventilated cold-roof nail bases that prevent heat loss from creating destructive ice dams.

  • The Heavy Wet "Cascade Concrete" Snowpack: Maritime snowpacks in the Pacific Northwest are warm and moisture-dense, weighing up to 30 to 40 pounds per cubic foot compared to light Rocky Mountain powder.
  • Unbalanced Snow Drift Sizing (ASCE 7-22): Wind scouring strips snow from windward roof slopes and redeposits it on leeward valleys, generating localized structural loading up to triple the nominal ground snow load.
  • Glulam Beam Sizing and Deflection Limits: Heavy structural timber glulams must be engineered with strict live-load deflection limits of $L/360$ or $L/480$ to prevent glass door binding and roof ponding.
  • The Ventilated Cold-Roof Nail Base: Installing a secondary ventilated roof deck above continuous structural insulation maintains a sub-freezing exterior roof deck, stopping ice dams at the source.

The Physics of Maritime Snow: "Cascade Concrete"

Building design in the mountainous interior of Washington, Oregon, Idaho, Montana, and Alaska requires a radical departure from lowland coastal building science. In lowland Seattle or Portland, structural roofs are engineered primarily for a nominal 25 PSF live load. However, as storms climb the windward slopes of Mount Hood, Stevens Pass, or Whitefish Mountain, atmospheric moisture precipitates as dense, saturated snowpack.

Unlike the dry, low-density powder snow characteristic of Colorado or Utah (which often weighs only 6 to 10 pounds per cubic foot), Pacific Northwest mountain snow is warm, maritime, and moisture-saturated. Regional ski patrol and civil engineers colloquially term this heavy snowpack "Cascade Concrete." Its density frequently exceeds 25 to 35 pounds per cubic foot when freshly fallen, compressing to over 45 pounds per cubic foot as rain-on-snow events occur throughout mid-winter.

A four-foot accumulation of dense Cascade snow resting atop a modest 2,000-square-foot mountain cabin roof represents a crushing physical deadweight of approximately 240,000 pounds—equivalent to parking sixty mid-size automobiles on the roof rafters. When warm Pacific atmospheric rivers drop warm winter rain on top of this accumulated snowpack, the snow absorbs the liquid water like an immense sponge without melting, instantly spiking the dead load beyond structural failure thresholds.

ASCE 7-22 Snow Loading Dynamics: Balanced vs. Unbalanced Drift

Structural roof engineering in high-snow jurisdictions is governed by Chapter 7 of the American Society of Civil Engineers standard ASCE 7-22 (Minimum Design Loads and Associated Criteria for Buildings and Other Structures). Structural engineers cannot simply size roof rafters for a uniform, flat blanket of snow. They must calculate the complex aerodynamic interaction between prevailing mountain winds and roof geometry.

During high-velocity mountain blizzard events, wind scours snow off the windward roof slope and carries it over the ridge. This scoured snow is deposited onto the sheltered leeward slope, creating massive, localized accumulations known as unbalanced snow drifts. The resulting load configuration places extreme rotational shear stresses on roof trusses:


In addition to windward-leeward drifting, valleys, dormers, and chimney projections create aerodynamic traps where snowdrifts can reach depths three to four times the nominal ground snow load. Roof trusses flanking roof valleys must be engineered with heavy multi-ply dimensional lumber or custom structural steel gusset plates to resist these intense localized point loads without catastrophic shear failure.

Glulam Beam Sizing and Structural Deflection Limits

For mountain cabins and timber lodges requiring wide, open floor plans with vaulted ceilings, Glued-Laminated Timber (Glulam) is the premier structural material. Manufactured by bonding dimensional Douglas fir lumber laminations under high hydraulic pressure with waterproof structural adhesives, glulams offer significantly higher bending stress ratings ($F_b \approx 2,400\ PSI$) and modulus of elasticity ($E \approx 1.8 \times 10^6\ PSI$) than standard sawn lumber.

When sizing glulam ridge beams and purlins for 100-PSF snow loads, the governing engineering criterion is rarely raw bending strength; it is structural deflection. If a roof beam deflects more than 1/2-inch over a 20-foot span under heavy winter snow, it causes adjacent window frames to bind, cracks interior plaster finishes, and creates low spots that prevent meltwater drainage.

Engineers specify strict deflection criteria of $L/360$ for live snow loads and $L/240$ for total combined dead-plus-live loads. For wide spans supporting heavy standing-seam metal roofs, specifying an ultra-stiff deflection threshold of $L/480$ is recommended. Modern glulam fabricators build structural camber (a slight upward parabolic curve) into the beam during manufacture, ensuring that when the heavy winter snowpack settles on the roof, the beam deflects into a perfectly flat, level plane.

The Cold-Roof Nail Base: Stopping Mountain Ice Dams

In high-elevation snow country, deep snow acts as a heavy insulating thermal blanket with an approximate insulating value of R-1 per inch. If heat from the living space escapes into the roof deck, it warms the bottom layer of the snowpack above the 32°F melting threshold. Meltwater trickles down the sloped roof beneath the snow blanket until it reaches the unheated exterior eaves, where freezing air transforms it into catastrophic ice dams.

To permanently stop ice dam formation on vaulted mountain roofs, building science mandates the construction of a ventilated cold roof. In this assembly, continuous rigid insulation (such as multiple layers of staggered polyisocyanurate or stone wool) is installed directly over the structural timber roof deck. Above this continuous insulation, technicians install 2x4 vertical sleeper furring strips aligned with the roof slope, creating a continuous 3.5-inch ventilation air cavity.

A secondary layer of 5/8-inch CDX plywood sheathing (the nail base) is then fastened over the sleepers to support the final standing seam metal roof covering. Cold mountain air enters continuous screened eave vents at the bottom, rushes up through the 3.5-inch cavity, and exhausts through an engineered high-capacity cold-roof ridge vent. This continuous convective wash keeps the exterior nail base at exact outdoor freezing temperatures, completely preventing the snowpack from melting from underneath.
