# Outboard Mineral Wool vs. Foam Rainscreens: Vapor Permeance & Wildfire Defense in Wet Climates

> **Quick Summary:** Modern building energy codes across the Pacific Northwest (including the Washington State Energy Code and Oregon Residential Specialty Code) mandate continuous exterior insulation (CI) to eliminate thermal bridging through wood wall studs. While petrochemical rigid foam boards (expanded polystyrene, extruded polystyrene, and polyisocyanurate) have traditionally dominated exterior retrofits, their low vapor permeance creates dangerous exterior condensation traps in Marine Zone 4C. Installing rigid stone wool (mineral wool) continuous insulation provides high outward vapor permeability exceeding 30 perms, allowing structural wall sheathing to dry outward continuously. Combined with Class A non-combustible wildfire resistance and zero long-term thermal drift, mineral wool establishes the gold standard for Cascadia rainscreen envelopes.
>
> - **The Outward Vapor Throttle Penalty:** Foil-faced polyisocyanurate and extruded polystyrene (XPS) act as Class I or II vapor barriers, trapping summer solar-driven inward moisture against structural OSB sheathing.
> - **Breathable Vapor Permeance:** Semi-rigid stone wool boards (such as Rockwool Comfortboard 110) offer an open, fibrous structure with vapor permeance exceeding 30 perms, promoting continuous outward drying.
> - **Thermal Drift and R-Value Stability:** Rigid polyiso foam loses insulating value during sub-freezing winter weather due to captive blowing agent condensation, whereas mineral wool maintains constant R-4.0 per inch across all temperatures.
> - **Non-Combustible Wildfire Defense:** In fire-prone Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) zones across Eastern Washington and Central Oregon, mineral wool withstands temperatures up to 2,150°F without melting or releasing toxic smoke.

## The Continuous Insulation Mandate in Northwest Energy Codes

Historically, American residential wall construction relied exclusively on "cavity insulation"—stuffing low-density fiberglass or mineral wool batts into the 3.5-inch or 5.5-inch voids between vertical 2x4 or 2x6 framing studs. While the insulation batts may carry a nominal rating of R-15 or R-21, wood framing members represent approximately 25% of the total exterior wall surface area. Because solid softwood framing conducts heat nearly four times faster than insulation, wood studs act as geometric thermal bridges that bypass cavity insulation.

To mitigate this conductive bypass, modern energy codes mandate **continuous exterior insulation (CI)** installed outboard of the structural wall sheathing. Adding R-5 to R-10 of continuous insulation blankets the structural framing, eliminating thermal bridging and elevating the temperature of the structural sheathing. By keeping the interior face of the OSB sheathing warm during cold winter months, continuous insulation prevents interior water vapor from condensing on cold structural wood.

However, selecting the correct material for this continuous outboard layer is critical to long-term envelope durability. In the maritime climate of Western Washington and Oregon, where exterior walls absorb substantial winter moisture, the insulation layer must not act as a vapor trap. Misapplying petrochemical rigid foam boards can inadvertently create moisture failures far more destructive than thermal bridging itself.

## Hygrothermal Physics: The Vapor Permeance Dilemma

The fundamental building science challenge of Marine Zone 4C is **drying potential**. When rainwater penetrates cladding through surface cracks or window trim seams, it must have an engineered pathway to dry out of the wall assembly. Historically, wall assemblies relied on drying inward toward the living space during summer months, or drying outward through breathable asphalt felt paper during winter.

When builders install 2-inch foil-faced polyisocyanurate (polyiso) or extruded polystyrene (XPS) over structural wall sheathing, they create an impermeable vapor barrier on the exterior of the building. This installation traps moisture within the wall cavity and stops outward drying:

```
[Interior Drywall] ---> [2x6 Wall Studs] ---> [OSB Sheathing] -X- [Foil-Faced Foam (0.03 Perms)]
                                                               ^
                                             (Total Vapor Blockage & Rot Trap)
```

Foil-faced polyisocyanurate has a vapor permeance rating of less than **0.03 perms**, classifying it as a strict Class I vapor barrier. If moisture penetrates behind the foam through screw holes or unsealed flashing seams, it is permanently trapped against the wood sheathing. Overcast marine weather provides zero drying heat, transforming the trapped water into an incubator for wood-rotting fungal decay.

Stone wool continuous insulation (manufactured from natural basalt rock and recycled steel slag) operates on the complete opposite thermodynamic principle. Rigid mineral wool boards possess an open-cell, fibrous micro-structure with a vapor permeance rating exceeding **30 to 40 perms**. It is completely "vapor-open," allowing moisture trapped inside the structural sheathing to diffuse freely into the ventilated rainscreen cavity and evaporate harmlessly.

## Cold-Temperature Thermal Drift: The Polyiso Defect

A major technical defect of rigid polyisocyanurate foam in cold climates is the phenomenon of **temperature-dependent thermal drift**. Polyiso foam achieves its high initial insulating rating (R-6.0 per inch) because microscopic gas bubbles trapped within the closed foam cells are filled with specialized chemical blowing agents (such as pentane blends) rather than air. Pentane gas has a lower thermal conductivity than atmospheric air.

However, fluid thermodynamics dictates that gases condense into liquids at specific temperatures. When outdoor temperatures plunge during winter cold snaps in Spokane, Bend, or Bozeman, the pentane blowing agent inside polyiso foam condenses from a gas into tiny liquid droplets on the internal cell walls. As a result, the foam cells are under vacuum, and conductive air takes over the heat transfer mechanism.

Extensive laboratory testing conducted by the Building Science Corporation demonstrates that when mean temperatures drop below 40°F, polyisocyanurate’s effective thermal resistance collapses from R-6.0 down to R-4.0 or lower. In contrast, rigid stone wool contains zero blowing agents; its insulating value is derived purely from trapped atmospheric air between non-conductive mineral fibers. As temperatures drop, molecular kinetic movement slows, causing mineral wool's effective R-value to actually increase in sub-zero winter cold.

## Wildfire WUI Non-Combustibility

In the fire-prone Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) corridors east of the Cascade Crest—including Central Oregon, Eastern Washington, and the Idaho Panhandle—exterior building materials must withstand intense wildfire ember storms. Petrochemical rigid foam boards are manufactured from synthetic petroleum polymers. When exposed to open flame or radiant heat during a wildfire, polystyrene foam softens, melts into flaming chemical liquid, and emits dense, toxic black smoke.

Stone wool continuous insulation is classified as **100% non-combustible** under ASTM E136 testing protocols. Formed from volcanic basalt rock melted in high-temperature blast furnaces at 2,900°F, mineral wool boards possess an ultimate melting point exceeding **2,150°F (1,177°C)**. It cannot ignite, support combustion, or produce toxic flame spread.

When installed behind non-combustible rainscreens (such as fiber cement lap or corrugated steel siding), exterior stone wool functions as an impenetrable heat shield that protects the interior wood framing from radiant heat ignition. Even if exterior siding boards crack or warp under direct flame contact, the mineral wool layer remains structurally intact, shielding the structural framing and allowing homes to survive extreme wildfire events.
